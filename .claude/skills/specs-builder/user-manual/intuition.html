Part 1: From zero

The big idea: facts and lines

This is the one idea the whole skill rests on. Once you have it, everything else follows.

What a graph is

Forget charts with bars. In this manual a graph just means dots joined by lines. You've used hundreds of them. A metro map is a graph: stations are dots, and tracks are lines. A family tree is another, and so is a list of who knows whom.

A graph is good at questions like what's connected to this? and how do I get from here to there? A long document is bad at those.

Now the idea: a specification can be a graph. Each fact is a dot. Each line says how two facts are connected.

Three words to carry with you

WordThink of it asIn your shop
NodeA dot: one fact, with a short nameR4 is a requirement, B1 a rule, SC3 a screen
LinkA line: how two facts are connectedR4 enforces B1
StaleA sticky note: please read this againR4 after B1 changes

The short names are called IDs. The letters tell you the kind: R for a requirement, B for a rule, SC for a screen. A number after a dot means a part of something: R4.2 is the second check on R4. After a day they read like street names.

Lines have a direction

This is the one idea worth slowing down for. Think of a house: the roof stands on the walls. Move a wall, and the roof may be in trouble. Repaint the roof, and the walls don't care.

A screen stands on a requirement in just this way. If the requirement changes, the screen may be wrong. So when something changes, the skill walks back along the lines to find everything standing on it. Those things go stale: not wrong, just needing a person to look again.

Under the hood

The facts are plain text files in a folder called data/, one to a node, so they can be kept and compared like any other files. A small database is rebuilt from them to make things fast, and can be thrown away at any time. That means there's no document to regenerate, and nothing that can fall behind.
