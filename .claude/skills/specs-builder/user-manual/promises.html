specs-builder user manual
Part 1: From zero

What the skill promises

Five promises the skill makes. Knowing them tells you what you can rely on.

One: a change can't half-happen

Think of a bank transfer: the money leaves one account and arrives in the other, or neither happens. Changes here work the same way. If a change would leave something broken, such as a line pointing at a fact that isn't there, it's refused whole. If it passes, it's logged: when, who and why.

Two: it can follow every connection

Because each fact has a name and each line has a meaning, you can ask things a document can't answer: why is this here, what shows it, what would a change reach, what isn't settled? The answers come from the lines, not from anyone's memory, so they can't be out of date.

Three: it fills in the gaps, but never makes up facts

When Claude writes, it adds the cases you didn't mention, such as what happens when a product is out of stock. Each one is marked as an addition until you confirm it. But numbers, laws and facts about your customers are asked for, never guessed.

Four: everything shows where it came from

A requirement is backed by notes of what was actually seen, each with a quote. The skill works out one word for how strong that backing is, and nobody can type it in by hand. See How well a requirement is backed.

Seeing the backing

Rule B1 is corroborated, because two sources agree: the running shop and the description for programmers. A wishlist, R15, is only documented: only your brief speaks of it. The application lists it, so someone can check.

Five: only you can say it's finished

When a spec is finished and accepted, it's agreed. That's possible only when nothing stands in the way: no unconfirmed addition, no unanswered question for you, nothing waiting to be read again. Even then, Claude won't agree it for you. Once agreed, it refuses every change until you reopen it.
